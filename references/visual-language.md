# Visual Language

## Direction

Aim for:
- archival;
- editorial;
- restrained;
- minimal;
- thoughtful;
- coherent surface treatment.

## Memory motif

Do not begin with a fixed vocabulary of lines, waves, eaves, bridges, or other
recurring examples. First identify the current photograph's source hierarchy:

1. the defining subject spirit or memory anchor;
2. the spatial rhythm, directional force, or tonal structure that makes the
   source perceptible without copying its outline;
3. atmosphere and material character;
4. secondary accent color or detail.

Select 3–6 source-specific facts from those levels. Prefer a distilled
watercolor re-expression over literal tracing. Aim for resemblance in spirit
rather than shape: use reduction, rhythm, mass, atmosphere, and color
relationships so the field feels like a memory summary, not a watercolor filter
or a recognizable redraw. Keep the main motif region roughly 35–50% of the
companion panel area, with an ideal target around 42–48%. The full internal
composition should feel optically scaled to about 60–68% visible load with the
rest preserved as calm ground. Do not shrink the motif into a small vignette;
below about 40% it usually loses authority.

The defining subject must remain present as a memory anchor, but not as a
complete copy. Use partial silhouettes, translucent washes, broken contours,
displaced echoes, reduced mass, symbolic traces, and source-derived rhythm.
The field should feel globally like the photograph while avoiding exact shape
copying. Do not solve abstraction by deleting the subject into generic
atmosphere.

Avoid isolated block layout. The title, journaling cue, and swatches may
occupy distinct functional positions, but they should remain visually
integrated with the memory field through shared spacing, tone, surface
texture, and subtle source-derived atmospheric echoes. Quiet areas may contain
pale wash residue, paper grain, diluted color drift, tiny hand-painted flecks,
or ghosted source-derived rhythm so they feel alive without competing with the
motif.

Source perceptibility is required; 1:1 subject reproduction is not allowed.
Avoid complete outlines, exact silhouettes, copied building facades, stair
geometry, tree placement, vehicle/animal forms, or landmark profiles. Translate
the source into mood, rhythm, density, direction, color memory, and atmospheric
pressure rather than recognizable object identity.

Prompt the motif through abstract cues, not literal object instructions. Use
phrases such as circular rhythm, vertical stillness, diagonal descent, clustered
warm light, reflected spacing, pale structural weight, or dusk density. Avoid
repeating the object's name as the thing to draw.

## Color

Use source-derived colors with a clear hierarchy:
1. emotional or memory color;
2. dark structural color;
3. pale neutral, air, light, or restrained accent.

The companion field may use related tonal steps for depth, but avoid unrelated
hues and muddy high-saturation accumulation. Use a light source-derived
atmospheric tint as the breathing-room ground, then check it against the
original photo as a complete diptych. Do not default every image to the same
beige paper; choose a restrained ground from the source's emotional
temperature, such as cool mist white, warm sunlit ivory, gray-green haze, pale
blue wash, snow white, or tea-warm paper. The two panels should share one
atmosphere and should not feel color-separated. Do not fill the field with an
unchanged, saturated source color.

## Swatches

Use exactly three visibly readable swatches. They should look like irregular
watercolor daubs or hand-brushed blocks with restrained opacity and soft,
natural edge variation. Keep them grouped and secondary, never as tiny UI
rectangles or decorative confetti. The group should occupy roughly 6–11% of
the companion panel width and remain legible at thumbnail size without drawing
attention before the motif. Swatches should feel embedded in the page's visual
flow, not like a separate palette card.

Select swatches by source role, but arrange the visible group by visual
harmony, not role order. Prefer a calm value sequence such as light-to-dark or
dark-to-light. Avoid light–dark–light, dark–light–dark, or
muted–bright–muted arrangements that make the group feel jumpy. If one swatch
is much brighter or more saturated than the others, place it at one end of the
sequence or reduce its size/opacity.

## Typography

Use one concise English title of 2–5 words. Prefer a legible handwritten
fountain-pen or ink-note style; keep letterforms calm and editorial rather than
ornate or novelty-script. Add at most one supporting subtitle or archive
caption when it improves meaning and balance.

Avoid oversized headlines and decorative typography. Keep the title below
roughly 35% of the companion panel width. Prefer the following fallback order:
legible fountain-pen handwriting, restrained humanist handwriting, then a
narrow editorial face. Never use ornate script, wedding-style lettering, or a
heavy display serif.

The journaling area may remain empty: use 2–4 widely spaced ruled lines,
baseline marks, or a blank note block as the intended writing cue. No invented
text is required. The lines should be low-contrast traces in the same field,
not a boxed writing section.

## Dynamic layout

Do not assign title, journaling lines, or swatches to a permanent
corner or side. First map the motif's occupied regions and quiet regions, then
place each element in the quiet space with optical balance. Auxiliary elements
must never overlap, cut through, or interrupt the key watercolor passage.

Use swatches and journaling lines as the last generated or placed balancing
marks. Their position should answer the motif's actual weight: for example, if
the motif gathers in the upper left, the calm lower left or lower middle may be
better than a default right-side placement. If the motif is low and broad, move
writing higher or farther into quiet ground. The goal is a composed page, not a
set of disconnected archive widgets.

When a quiet zone feels too empty, do not fill it with a new subject or larger
typography. Add only subtle connective atmosphere: a diluted wash edge, faint
rhythmic residue, sparse flecks, or a soft tonal drift derived from the source.

If the field lacks usable quiet space, simplify the motif or rebuild the whole
panel composition before adding typography. Use a soft warm-white or cool-white
ground that matches the original photograph's color temperature. A quiet white
ground is preferable to crowding or forced placement.

If the image supports a specific location or landmark with approximately 99%
confidence, a small archival location note may be added. If not, omit it.
