# Visual Language

## Direction

Aim for work that feels:

- archival;
- editorial;
- restrained;
- tactile;
- quiet;
- coherent as one watercolor page.

## Source memory

First identify 3–6 image-specific facts:

1. defining subject or memory anchor;
2. dominant spatial rhythm, axis, or mass relationship;
3. atmosphere, light, and material character;
4. secondary accent color or detail.

Translate those facts into watercolor reduction, broken contour, translucent
wash, partial silhouette, displaced echo, or rhythmic marks.

Keep one or two source-specific structural relationships perceptible. For
example, retain a building’s overall light-and-shadow mass, a tree canopy’s
pressure, a figure’s gesture, or a landscape’s horizon relation. Do not copy
every window, branch, face, object count, or exact outline.

The goal is resemblance in spirit with a small amount of recognizable structure,
not literal replication and not generic abstraction.

## Motif and ground

Keep the primary motif roughly 42–48% of the panel. Use a restrained,
source-derived paper ground for the remaining quiet space.

Do not default every image to beige. Choose a pale atmosphere related to the
source: cool mist white, warm ivory, gray-green haze, pale blue wash, snow
white, or tea-warm paper.

Use subtle paper fibers, diluted wash edges, tonal drift, or quiet echo marks to
make calm space feel alive. Do not add unrelated symbols or decorative patterns.

## Archive elements

Generate title, journaling lines, and swatches with the companion panel.

- Use exactly three source-derived watercolor daubs.
- Keep swatches grouped, visible, and secondary.
- Prefer light-to-dark or dark-to-light ordering.
- Use one concise legible handwritten English title.
- Use 2–4 empty ruled lines with no journal prose.
- Do not add captions, subtitles, dates, place names, seals, labels, or logos.

All archive elements should share the same paper texture, tone, and visual
weather as the motif.
